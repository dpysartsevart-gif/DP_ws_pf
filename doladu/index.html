The expensive part

The problem was never
the modelling.

It's the fifty small things between “done” and “delivered”. A normal map in the wrong colour space. Two textures at 4K that should be 1K. A pivot that isn't at the origin. Naming that passes your eye and fails the importer.

None of them are hard to fix. All of them are hard to find — and you find them at the worst possible moment, in someone else's engine, with your name on the file.

One pass

One scan.
One number.
Then the list.

Point it at a project folder. It reads every mesh and every texture once, then scores the project out of 100 across naming, normals, UVs, texel density, texture sizes and colour space, duplicates, pivots, scale, collision meshes and lightmap UVs.

The score is the headline. The list under it is the work — every finding names the file, says what's wrong, and offers the fix as a button.

The difference

It fixes what it found.

Repack ORM. Convert rough ↔ gloss. Resize to power-of-two. Rename to the UE convention. Move pivots to origin. Flip a normal map's green channel. Batch-convert formats.

Every destructive step is journalled. One command puts it all back — including the batch you ran twenty minutes ago.

Safe mode is on by default: results go to copies, your originals are never touched until you say so.

What one scan covers

Ten checks,
one pass over the folder.

Files checked against a naming profile — prefixes, texture suffixes, packed maps — with auto-rename for everything that doesn't conform. Duplicate assets and oversized files surfaced in the same pass, so you find the copy of a copy before it ships with the rest.
Winding consistency, so flipped faces are named rather than discovered in engine. Green channel direction for DirectX against OpenGL, with a before/after preview. Colour space per map — a normal map sitting in sRGB is caught here, not in a lighting review.
UV channel validation with the UDIM grid, overlap detection, and a texel density heatmap across the mesh measured against your target rather than against a guess. Lightmap UVs checked separately, because the engine treats them separately.
Pivot position against origin, units and bounding box in centimetres, up-axis read from the geometry rather than assumed, and collision meshes checked for the naming and shape the engine expects. A units and axis reference for twelve programs sits beside it, because that mismatch is where most broken exports begin.
Texture sizes against what the asset actually needs, power-of-two compliance, clipped blacks and whites, and total VRAM for the set measured against a budget you set. Full-depth EXR and 16-bit textures go through OpenImageIO, so a 16-bit normal map stays 16-bit.

Moodboard

Reference boards
that do something.

Four boards per project. Drop images, notes, colour tags and strokes on an infinite canvas.

Every board extracts its own palette and exports .ase — the swatches open straight in Photoshop and Substance. The colour tags carry the hex, and you can select the code and copy it.

It's the reference wall you already keep, except it lives with the project and feeds the rest of the app.
